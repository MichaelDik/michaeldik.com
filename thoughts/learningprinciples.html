Learning Principles

learning · November 2025 · 6 min read

These are the principles I default to whenever I need to get good at something new—whether it is a technical topic, a product area, or a physical skill. The list is short on theory and heavy on things I actually do.

1) Make it project-shaped

I learn fastest when there is a concrete output and a deadline. I pick a project just beyond my current ability and ship it in public to create stakes.

  • Small, scoped projects beat vague studying
  • Shipping forces you to close gaps you would otherwise ignore
  • Public work creates accountability and feedback

2) Tight feedback loops

The faster I get signal, the faster I learn. I look for ways to see if something worked within minutes or hours, not weeks.

  • Code: tests, linters, small PRs, quick demos
  • Writing: share drafts early with one or two people
  • Speaking: rehearse with a timer, record and review

3) Depth before breadth

I try to understand one representative example deeply before skimming everything else. Master one end-to-end path, then fan out.

  • Follow one tutorial or doc all the way through
  • Rebuild it from memory without notes
  • Only then explore variations and edge cases

4) Retrieval and spaced reps

Re-reading is slow; retrieval is fast. I favor flashcards for facts and short recall exercises for concepts, spaced over days.

  • Write down the minimal fact; test yourself, do not just review
  • Spacing beats cramming—short sessions across the week
  • Delete cards that are easy; keep the deck lean

5) Teach to learn

If I cannot explain it, I do not know it. I write short summaries or Looms aimed at a specific person and refine until the explanation is crisp.

  • Explain without jargon first; add detail later
  • Note where the explanation feels fuzzy—that is where to study next
  • Store the explainer so future-me can reuse it

6) Energy and environment

Learning is expensive. I front-load the hard work into my highest-energy hours and make the environment boring so focus is easy.

  • Block 2–3 hour deep-work windows; put the phone in another room
  • Standarize tools to reduce friction (same editor, same notebook)
  • Sleep, movement, sunlight—obvious but non-negotiable

Putting it together

Pick a project, set a deadline, design a feedback loop, and schedule deep work for it. Capture what you learn, teach it once, and keep a few flashcards for the parts that need to stick. That is the whole playbook.
