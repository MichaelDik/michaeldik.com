Frequently Asked Questions About Writing and Learning Online

writing · 10 min read

I occasionally get questions about my approach to learning, writing, and sharing online. While I'm still figuring things out myself, I've consolidated some thoughts here that might be helpful if you're considering starting to write online.

This FAQ covers:

  • How I got started writing online
  • Why I write and share publicly
  • My writing process and tools
  • Advice for getting started
  • Balancing writing with a full-time job

Getting Started

How did you get started writing online?

I started writing primarily as a learning tool. Early in my career, I realized that writing about what I was learning helped me understand it more deeply. The act of explaining something forces you to organize your thoughts and identify gaps in your understanding.

Initially, I just kept notes for myself. But I noticed that when I shared these notes with colleagues, they found them useful. That encouraged me to start publishing more publicly. The feedback loop of sharing → getting feedback → learning more → sharing again has been incredibly valuable.

What made you decide to write publicly instead of keeping private notes?

A few reasons:

  • Accountability: Publishing publicly adds a bit of pressure to make your writing clearer and more accurate
  • Serendipity: You never know who might find your writing useful or what conversations it might spark
  • Future reference: It's easier to share a link than to dig through private notes when someone asks a question
  • Building in public: Showing your work creates opportunities for feedback and connection
"The best way to learn is to teach."

Why I Write

Who do you write for?

First and foremost, I write for myself. Writing is my way of thinking through problems and consolidating what I'm learning. If I'm interested in a topic, writing about it helps me understand it better.

But I also write for:

  • My colleagues: Sharing what I've learned with my team and others in similar roles
  • People earlier in their journey: The things I'm learning now might be helpful for someone a few steps behind me
  • My future self: I often come back to things I've written and think "oh yeah, that's how that works"

What do you write about?

I typically write about things I'm actively working on or learning about:

  • Technology and product development
  • Lessons from books I'm reading
  • Problem-solving approaches that worked (or didn't)
  • Tools and workflows that improve productivity

The common thread is that I'm writing about things I'm genuinely curious about and spending time on. This makes writing much easier because I'm already immersed in the topic.

Writing Process

What's your writing process?

My process is pretty straightforward:

  1. Collect notes: As I'm learning something, I take notes in a simple text editor. These are usually bullet points, quotes, and rough thoughts.
  2. Identify patterns: After accumulating notes, I look for themes or insights worth sharing.
  3. Create an outline: I organize my notes into a rough outline with section headers.
  4. Write a rough draft: I expand the outline into full paragraphs, focusing on getting ideas down rather than perfection.
  5. Edit for clarity: I read through the draft, simplifying sentences and removing jargon where possible.
  6. Publish: I set a deadline and ship it, knowing I can always update it later.

What tools do you use?

I keep things simple:

  • Note-taking: Plain text files or Apple Notes for quick capture
  • Writing: VS Code or any text editor—I write in HTML/Markdown
  • Publishing: Static HTML hosted on GitHub Pages

The tools don't really matter. What matters is having a frictionless way to capture ideas and publish them. The easier it is, the more likely you'll actually do it.

How long does it take you to write a post?

It varies widely depending on the topic and depth:

  • Quick posts: 1-2 hours for shorter pieces or book summaries
  • In-depth articles: 5-10 hours spread over multiple days
  • Technical deep dives: Can take weeks if I'm learning the topic while writing

But remember: the time spent writing is also time spent learning. It's not just writing time—it's learning time.

Getting Started

I want to start writing, but I don't think I'm an expert in anything. Should I still write?

Absolutely yes! This is one of the biggest myths about writing online—that you need to be an expert.

Here's the thing: wherever you are in your learning journey, there are people ahead of you and behind you. You can learn from those ahead while helping those behind. Your "beginner" perspective might actually be more helpful to other beginners than an expert's perspective.

Some of my most popular posts have been things like "Here's what I learned this week" or "I tried X and here's what happened." You don't need to be the world's foremost expert—you just need to share honestly about what you're learning.

How do I overcome perfectionism and actually publish?

This is something I still struggle with, but here's what helps:

  • Set a deadline: Decide when you'll publish and stick to it. Done is better than perfect.
  • Remember you can update: Publishing isn't final. You can always improve something later.
  • Lower the stakes: You're not writing for the New York Times. You're sharing notes on the internet.
  • Focus on value: Instead of asking "Is this perfect?" ask "Will this help someone?"

What should I write about?

Write about what genuinely interests you. The best writing comes from genuine curiosity and enthusiasm. Ask yourself:

  • What am I currently learning?
  • What problems have I recently solved?
  • What do people frequently ask me about?
  • What do I wish someone had explained to me a year ago?

Balancing Writing and Work

How do you find time to write while working full-time?

I treat writing as part of my learning process, not as something separate from my work. Often, I'm writing about things directly related to what I'm doing at work (without sharing proprietary information, of course).

Some practical tips:

  • Write as you learn: Take notes while learning something new at work, then clean them up for publishing
  • Use small pockets of time: Even 30 minutes can be productive for drafting or editing
  • Batch similar tasks: Do research in one session, writing in another, editing in another
  • Don't force it: If you're not feeling it, don't write. Quality over quantity

Final Thoughts

Any final advice for someone starting out?

Just start. Pick something you're interested in, write 300 words about it, and publish it. It doesn't need to be revolutionary or comprehensive—it just needs to be honest and helpful.

The hardest part is hitting "publish" the first time. After that, it gets easier. You'll develop your voice, figure out what resonates, and most importantly, you'll learn a ton.

Remember: you're playing a long game. Don't worry about going viral or building a huge audience immediately. Focus on consistently sharing what you're learning, and good things will follow.

"The best time to start was yesterday. The second best time is now."
